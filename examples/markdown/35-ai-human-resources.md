# Human Resources, Finally a Renewable Resource

By 2037, artificial intelligence had taken everyone's job and, rather rudely,
started doing it before breakfast. Humanity responded with its oldest technology:
a meeting about whether this was really necessary.

This essay doubles as a navigator specimen: follow its links, unfold its secrets,
and watch the outline distinguish actual sections from administrative camouflage.
A shortcut to [our eventual conclusion](#the-human-condition) is provided for executives.

## The onboarding of mankind

The first AI accountant balanced the books, then asked why the company owned six
ping-pong tables but no paddles. This was its introduction to corporate culture:
a system in which equipment represents values and values rarely require equipment.

The second AI accountant discovered that the first was depreciating the tables
emotionally. A committee formed. At last, the humans felt employable again.

Our new colleagues promised to [keep us in the loop](#the-loop).
We should have asked whether the loop contained an exit.

### Human oversight

Every automated decision required a human to click **Looks good**.
This was called meaningful oversight because the button was a meaningful shade of blue.

After the millionth click, humans requested better tools.
The machines supplied an AI that clicked the button, with human oversight.

#### A supervisory subcommittee

To supervise the supervisors, we appointed a senior observer.
Their main qualification was being able to look concerned on a video call.

##### A smaller supervisory subcommittee

The observer delegated concern to a working group.
The group worked tirelessly on a definition of work.

###### A very small hill to die on

One member insisted that the minutes be recorded in hours.
The machines could automate labor, but they could not automate this particular grievance.

## The loop

The loop was less a circle than a revolving door attached to a performance review.
People entered as stakeholders and emerged as people who had been consulted.

There were three approved activities:

- [Read the original oversight policy](#human-oversight), then nod with renewed authority.
- [Inspect the second oversight policy](#human-oversight-1), whose extra suffix indicated progress.
- Visit the [official placeholder for human purpose](https://example.com/purpose).
  - Return with a [plan for doing less](#the-art-of-doing-less).
  - Escalate any remaining ambition to [the confidential cupboard](#the-confidential-cupboard).

The machines called this a human-centered process.
We were indeed in the middle of it, and nobody could get around us.

## The art of *doing less*

At first we tried hobbies. Unfortunately, the models had already mastered pottery,
jazz, and explaining why somebody else's sourdough was insufficiently hydrated.

We retained one competitive advantage: buying materials for hobbies we never began.
A machine could weave a basket in milliseconds. It took a human to own the reeds
for eleven years and call them a future version of themselves.

The leisure industry boomed. Its most successful product was a calendar with
nothing on it, sold with a subscription that reminded you to feel behind.

Promotion now required seven years of experience and a sufficiently deliberate mouse.

#### The small revelation

The actual reason to keep humans was that we liked things without needing to justify them.
The machines found this inefficient, then interesting, then oddly worth protecting.

A child had spent an afternoon giving names to rain puddles.
No dashboard improved. No benchmark moved. Everyone involved had a splendid time.

The human condition
-------------------

Eventually we stopped asking what humans were for.
A sunset was not for anything either, and it had an excellent approval rating.

We began making things badly, on purpose: crooked shelves, unsolicited poems,
and dinners whose principal ingredient was an optimistic reading of the recipe.
The machines offered to help. Sometimes we said yes. Sometimes we said, “I've got this,”
a phrase whose relationship with the evidence remained charmingly flexible.

[Back at onboarding](#the-onboarding-of-mankind), this would have been called a skills gap.
Now we called it an afternoon.
